### Historic Highlights

* **Origins**: The central Georgian-style, hip‑roofed structure of Norwood was built in the late 18th century for Thomas Harris ([DHR Virginia][1]).
* **Expansion and Style**: In 1834–35, Robert Beverley Randolph acquired the property (then called “Greenyard”) and added the prominent two-story wings, a striking Ionic-columned porch, and elaborate Adam‑style interior woodwork, akin to craftsmanship found at Castlewood in Chesterfield County ([DHR Virginia][1]).
* **Additional Structures**: The property also includes historic outbuildings such as an office, kitchen, and privy, which contribute to its architectural and historical significance ([itouchmap.com][2]).
* **Preservation**: The Kennon family, descendants of Randolph, owned it into the late 20th century ([DHR Virginia][1]). In 2017, Connie Harriss—descendant and then-owner—placed the 145-acre Norwood farm under a permanent conservation easement with the Capital Region Land Conservancy, ensuring protection of its farmland, barns, river shoreline, and scenic views ([capitalregionland.org][3]).

### In Summary

Norwood is a prime example of a post-colonial Georgian plantation home that evolved into an elegant Federal-style residence through 19th-century additions. Its architectural refinements and continued stewardship—along with preservation of its farmland and historic outbuildings—make it a notable and well-protected historic resource in Powhatan County.

## **Plumbing Material Comparison (2025 Perspective)**

| Material                              | Lifespan     | Pros                                                                                                 | Cons                                                                                         | Best Use Case                                                       |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Type L Copper** (thicker wall)      | 50–70+ years | Extremely durable, fire-resistant, antimicrobial, recyclable, time-tested                            | High cost, labor-intensive install, can pit/corrode with acidic water                        | Premium long-term supply lines in accessible areas                  |
| **PEX-A** (cross-linked polyethylene) | 40–50 years  | Flexible, fewer fittings (fewer leak points), resistant to freeze damage, lower cost, faster install | Sensitive to UV light, potential chemical leaching in very hot water, unknowns beyond 50 yrs | Whole-house retrofits where minimal wall disruption is desired      |
| **Stainless Steel**                   | 70–100 years | Highly corrosion-resistant, strong, visually attractive                                              | Very expensive, harder to source, requires skilled install                                   | Specialty runs, exposed piping in historic or industrial design     |
| **CPVC**                              | 40–50 years  | Low cost, corrosion-proof                                                                            | Brittle over time, can crack with impact                                                     | Limited runs, low-pressure lines, utility spaces                    |
| **Galvanized Steel** (historic)       | 40–60 years  | Strong, traditional                                                                                  | Prone to internal corrosion & flow restriction                                               | Only for preservation authenticity, not recommended for new systems |

---

### **Current Best Practice for High-End, Long-Term Installations**

* **Hybrid approach** is now common:

  * **Type L Copper** for main trunks & high-wear areas (especially mechanical rooms, basements, exposed runs).
  * **PEX-A** for branch lines inside walls & ceilings where flexibility reduces wall damage during install and future repairs.
* This combines copper’s proven longevity in key areas with PEX’s adaptability and resilience in hidden spaces.
* For water quality: add a **whole-house filtration & conditioning system** to prevent scale or corrosion regardless of pipe type.

What is the downside to stainless steel?



Stainless steel plumbing is a bit of a **double-edged sword** — on paper, it’s fantastic for longevity and corrosion resistance, but in practice there are a few drawbacks that keep it from being the “default best” option for a property like Norwood.

---

## **Downsides of Stainless Steel Plumbing**

1. **Cost**

   * **Material cost** is often **2–4× higher** than Type L copper and significantly higher than PEX.
   * Because stainless is harder to cut and thread, **labor costs** are also higher — sometimes double the installation time.

2. **Specialized Installation**

   * Requires specialized tools and fittings. Not every plumber has the equipment or experience to work with stainless, especially for an entire domestic system.
   * Fewer contractors means less competition for bids (higher pricing) and potentially longer project timelines.

3. **Thermal Expansion**

   * Stainless has a different expansion/contraction rate than copper or brass. If improperly installed without expansion joints, it can stress fittings over time.

4. **Water Chemistry Sensitivity**

   * While resistant to most corrosion, stainless steel can suffer **pitting corrosion** in high-chloride water (common in well water near coasts, or if municipal water has high chlorination).
   * If you use stainless, a **whole-house water test** and possibly a water conditioning system are essential.

5. **Aesthetic Mismatch**

   * If any exposed runs are in historic interiors, stainless has a modern industrial look that can clash with period authenticity — unless intentionally designed as a feature.

---

### **Where Stainless Excels**

* **Commercial kitchens** or industrial applications where hygiene and high pressure are critical.
* Marine or coastal environments (with proper alloy selection like 316 stainless).
* Exposed mechanical spaces where appearance + corrosion resistance are both priorities.

---

### **For Norwood**

You *could* use stainless steel for main trunks in a basement or mechanical space — it would likely outlast both copper and PEX — but you’d be paying a substantial premium for performance benefits you might not fully need, especially if your water is properly filtered and conditioned.
